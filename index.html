Jordan, 34, mixed martial arts coach. Jordan presents to your primary-contact physiotherapy clinic with a three-week history of deep right groin pain. It began after an intensive grappling camp involving repeated pivoting and forced hip flexion. The pain is worse when Jordan drops into a deep squat or sits with the knee crossed over, and there is occasional clicking in the groin. Jordan reports no fever, no night sweats and is able to weight-bear and walk into the clinic, though a limp is present after training. Past history includes two courses of oral corticosteroids for asthma this year and moderate weekly alcohol intake. On examination, hip flexion and internal rotation are reduced and reproduce the groin pain, and the FADIR test is positive. Observations taken in clinic are unremarkable. Jordan is keen to keep coaching and wants to know what is going on and whether a scan is needed.
Row 3 · MC · 1 pt
Jordan is 34, presents with three weeks of deep right groin pain after a grappling camp, and reports two recent courses of oral corticosteroids and moderate alcohol intake. Weight-bearing is possible, and there is no fever or night sweats. Given the full picture, what is the most appropriate reasoning about red flags at this first visit?
A. Septic arthritis is the priority concern and warrants urgent referral before any physiotherapy assessment.
Septic arthritis is a genuine hip red flag, but the slide lists its picture as severe pain, fever and inability to weight-bear. Jordan is afebrile and weight-bearing, so this does not fit. It would become the priority if Jordan developed fever with a hot, very painful hip and could no longer bear weight.
B. No red-flag pathology need be considered, because the mechanical history and positive FADIR fully explain the presentation.
The mechanical story and positive FADIR do point strongly to an intra-articular problem, but dismissing red flags entirely misses the corticosteroid and alcohol history, which are specific AVN risk factors. Safe primary-contact practice keeps AVN on the list even when a mechanical cause looks likely.
C. Avascular necrosis should be kept in mind given the corticosteroid and alcohol history and the deep, progressive groin pain, so response to management should be monitored.
Correct. AVN risk on the slide is steroids, alcohol and trauma, and the presentation is deep, progressive groin pain, which matches Jordan's corticosteroid courses, alcohol intake and groin pain. AVN does not need emergency referral like septic arthritis, so keeping it in mind and monitoring the response to management is the appropriate call. Revisit the "Red Flags – Primary Contact Practice" slide.
D. The recent corticosteroid use points to septic arthritis, so inflammatory markers and urgent referral are the immediate priority.
This mixes up two red flags. Corticosteroids raise AVN risk, not septic arthritis risk, and septic arthritis is signalled by fever and inability to weight-bear, which are absent here. Urgent referral for sepsis would be right only if those infective features were present.
General feedback: Two hip red flags appear on the slide: AVN (risk from steroids, alcohol, trauma; deep progressive groin pain) and septic arthritis (severe pain, fever, inability to weight-bear, needing urgent referral). Jordan carries AVN risk factors but none of the septic picture, so the reasoning is to keep AVN in mind and monitor, not to trigger an emergency referral.
Row 4 · MC · 1 pt
Jordan, the 34-year-old coach, has reduced hip flexion and internal rotation that reproduce the groin pain, a positive FADIR test, clicking, and symptoms brought on by deep squatting and sitting with the knee crossed. Which interpretation of these findings is best supported?
A. The global restriction across all hip movements points to established hip osteoarthritis.
OA does give global range restriction, but the slide ties OA to older age and lists morning stiffness and buttock/groin pain, and the pattern here is a selective loss of flexion and internal rotation, not a global one. At 34 with a positive FADIR, impingement fits far better.
B. The reduced flexion and internal rotation with a positive FADIR fit femoroacetabular impingement, with possible labral involvement given the clicking and pivoting history.
Correct. The FAI clinical features slide lists reduced hip flexion and internal rotation and a positive FADIR, which is exactly Jordan's pattern, and the clicking plus pivoting history raises labral involvement, noted on the slides as often occurring with FAI. This connects the examination pattern to the most likely intra-articular diagnosis. Revisit the "FAI – Clinical Features" and "Labral Tears" slides.
C. Morning stiffness lasting under 30 minutes is the key feature and indicates early osteoarthritis.
Morning stiffness under 30 minutes is an OA feature on the slide, but nothing in the case reports morning stiffness, and Jordan's age and impingement signs point away from OA. This option latches onto a single OA cue that is not actually present.
D. The clicking alone confirms an isolated traumatic labral tear independent of any impingement.
Clicking can accompany a labral tear, but the slides describe labral tears as often degenerative and linked with FAI or dysplasia, not as isolated findings confirmed by clicking alone. The positive FADIR and restricted flexion and internal rotation point to impingement driving the picture.
General feedback: FAI shows as reduced flexion and internal rotation with a positive FADIR, and labral tears commonly sit alongside it, especially with a pivoting mechanism and clicking. The examination pattern here is selective rather than global, which separates FAI from OA.
Row 5 · MC · 1 pt
Jordan, the 34-year-old coach with a positive FADIR and clicking, wants to know whether a scan is needed and what it would show. You suspect FAI with possible labral involvement. Which imaging choice is the most appropriate to confirm the suspected labral pathology, and why?
A. Plain X-ray, because it is the gold standard for demonstrating labral tears.
X-ray is the right tool for OA changes and FAI bony morphology on the slide, but it does not show soft-tissue labral tears and is not the gold standard for them. It would be a reasonable first film for the bony picture, not for confirming a labral tear.
B. MRI without contrast, because it is the definitive test for cam and pincer bony morphology.
MRI does image labral and chondral tissue, but the slide names cam and pincer morphology as an X-ray finding, and the gold standard for labral pathology is the MRI arthrogram, not plain MRI. This option pairs the right modality with the wrong indication.
C. No imaging is useful, because intra-articular hip pathology cannot be visualised.
The imaging slide clearly maps modalities to intra-articular pathology, so saying nothing is useful contradicts the source. Labral and chondral lesions are exactly what MRI and MRI arthrogram are used to show.
D. MRI arthrogram, because it is the gold standard for labral pathology, while X-ray remains useful for FAI morphology and OA.
Correct. The imaging slide lists the MRI arthrogram as the gold standard for labral pathology, with X-ray for OA and FAI morphology and MRI for labral and chondral lesions. Choosing the arthrogram to confirm the suspected labral tear, while noting X-ray still has a role for the bony picture, matches the source and Jordan's suspected diagnosis. Revisit the "Imaging & Diagnosis" slide.
General feedback: Match the modality to the target: X-ray for OA and FAI bony morphology, MRI for labral and chondral lesions, and MRI arthrogram as the gold standard for labral pathology. For a suspected labral tear, the arthrogram is the confirmatory choice.
